# Idea validation framework

Validating an idea means finding evidence for or against it before spending large amounts of time or money. A useful evaluation looks at six dimensions: technical feasibility, market opportunity, competition, business model, resource requirements, and execution complexity.

## Step 1: State the idea clearly
Write one sentence in the form: "We help [target users] who struggle with [problem] by providing [solution], unlike [current alternatives]." If any part is missing, the idea is not yet clear enough to evaluate.

## Step 2: Check the problem
- Who exactly has the problem, and how often?
- How do they solve it today, and what does that cost them in money, time, or frustration?
- Is the problem painful enough that people would change their behaviour or pay?
Strong signals: people already spend money or effort on workarounds, they complain publicly, or they ask for solutions. Weak signals: friends say "good idea", or the problem is only mentioned when prompted.

## Step 3: Check the market
Estimate the number of potential customers in a clearly defined segment, then how many are reachable at the start. Prefer a bottom-up estimate (number of customers multiplied by expected price) over a top-down claim such as "1% of a huge market".

## Step 4: Check competition
Direct competitors solve the same problem the same way. Indirect competitors solve it differently, including doing nothing or using spreadsheets. Existing competitors often prove demand. The question is whether there is a segment they serve poorly and whether the idea can be meaningfully different.

## Step 5: Check feasibility and resources
Can the core feature be built with available technology and skills? Which parts depend on third parties, data, regulation, or scale? Estimate team, time, and cost for a first version.

## Step 6: Test with real people
Interview potential customers, run a landing page test, or offer a manual version of the service before building software. Record what people do, not only what they say.

## Common red flags
- No clear target customer.
- Dependence on one risky assumption that has not been tested.
- A large market claim with no way to reach the first customers.
- No difference from competitors beyond "better" or "cheaper".
- A plan that needs everything built before any user feedback.

## Reporting evidence honestly
Separate verified facts (backed by a source), assumptions (needed but unverified), and suggestions (opinions). State a confidence level. Scores are estimates to guide decisions, not predictions of success.
